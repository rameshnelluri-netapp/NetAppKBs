What MTU actually is

MTU — Maximum Transmission Unit — is the largest IP packet that one specific link will carry without needing to break it into pieces. It's a property of a single hop, not of "the network" as a whole, and that one distinction is the root of almost everything in this guide.

Every interface on every device — your laptop's Wi‑Fi adapter, a router's WAN port, a VPN tunnel's virtual interface — has its own MTU setting. The overwhelmingly common default, inherited from classic Ethernet, is 1500 bytes. That number measures the IP packet itself: the IP header, whatever transport header sits inside it (TCP, UDP…), and the actual payload — not the Ethernet header and trailer wrapped around it for delivery on the wire.

Why this matters before anything else

Your machine only knows its own interface's MTU. It has no built‑in way of knowing the MTU of every router, tunnel, and link between it and whatever it's talking to. The entire rest of this guide is really about that one gap — and what happens when nothing fills it in.

02 / 10

Where 1500 bytes came from

It's not an arbitrary number, and it's not a hard physical limit either — it's a 1980s engineering trade-off that's stuck around for forty years because changing it everywhere at once is harder than living with it.

Classic Ethernet had no error correction — a single corrupted bit meant discarding the whole frame and retransmitting it. Bigger frames meant more retransmitted data per error; smaller frames meant more header overhead as a percentage of every byte sent, and worse multiplexing fairness, since one large frame could make every other device on a shared segment wait its turn. 1500 bytes was the balance point the standard settled on, and because Ethernet became close to universal, that number got baked into operating systems, routers, and applications everywhere — to the point that changing a single device's MTU today doesn't change anyone else's.

Where every link on a path is under one operator's control — inside a single data center, across a dedicated storage network, sometimes across a cloud provider's own backbone — networks often raise this to 9000‑byte "jumbo frames" instead, trading the old fairness/error concerns (largely solved by modern hardware) for far less per-byte header overhead. The catch, and the setup for the rest of this guide, is that jumbo frames only work if literally every hop on the path agrees to them — one normal-MTU hop in the middle breaks the whole plan.

03 / 10

Path MTU: the weakest link wins

The MTU that actually matters for a given connection isn't your MTU, or the destination's MTU — it's the smallest MTU of any single hop in between, and you usually can't see that number from either end.

A packet travels across a chain of independent links to reach its destination, and each one can have a different MTU. The Path MTU for that specific route is simply the minimum of all of them. Common reasons a hop somewhere in the middle ends up smaller than the usual 1500: PPPoE links (their own encapsulation overhead typically caps them at 1492), and — the case this guide keeps coming back to — tunnels. IPsec, GRE, WireGuard, VXLAN, and similar protocols all wrap your original packet inside another header (and sometimes a trailer and integrity check too), and that overhead comes straight out of the usable payload. A tunnel commonly lands somewhere around 1400–1450 bytes of effective MTU, exactly the kind of gap shown in the diagram at the top of this page.
